# L03-P04 & L03-P05 — Serial Position Effect and Von Restorff Effect

---

## Serial Position Effect (L03-P04)

**Evidence level:** A | **Type:** Empirical effect | **Domain:** Memory, information retention

### Definition
Items presented first (primacy) and items presented last (recency) in a sequence are remembered better than items presented in the middle.

### Research Basis
The serial position effect was established by Murdock (1962) and has extensive support in memory research. Primacy is attributed to items entering long-term memory; recency to items remaining in working memory at time of recall.

### UI Implications
1. **Place the most important navigation items first and last.** Middle navigation items are less reliably recalled and noticed.
2. **Place primary actions at the beginning or end of action sequences.** Confirmation, submit, and primary CTAs benefit from recency positioning.
3. **Onboarding sequences: lead with the most memorable value proposition.** The first impression (primacy) and final screen (recency) will be remembered best.
4. **In dropdown lists and select menus, organize so frequently needed items appear at the top or are searchable.** Avoid burying common options in the middle of long lists.
5. **Tab bars (mobile):** Place high-priority destinations at position 1 (primacy) or position 5/last (recency) — not positions 2, 3, or 4.

### Anti-Patterns
❌ Placing the most important action (e.g., "Submit Application") in the middle of a footer with equal weight to tertiary links.

❌ Long dropdown menus with the most-used option buried at position 15 of 30.

## Von Restorff Effect (L03-P05)

**Evidence level:** A | **Type:** Empirical effect | **Domain:** Memory, perceptual distinctiveness

### Definition
An item that is visually or contextually distinct from surrounding items is more likely to be noticed and remembered than items that are similar to each other.

### Research Basis
Originally described by Hedwig von Restorff (1933). The isolation effect — that distinctive items are better remembered — has been replicated extensively in memory research.

### UI Implications
1. **Primary CTAs should be visually distinct from all other buttons on the page.** A filled, brightly colored primary button surrounded by ghost/outline secondary buttons is a direct application.
2. **Use distinctiveness deliberately, not randomly.** Every element cannot be distinctive — if everything stands out, nothing does.
3. **Notification badges, unread indicators, and status alerts use the Von Restorff effect.** The red circle on a notification badge is distinctive by color in a neutral context.
4. **Highlighted states, featured products, and recommended options use distinctiveness.** One visually prominent item in a list will be noticed first.
5. **Do not apply visual distinction without semantic intent.** A randomly bolded word in body text creates distinction without meaning.

### Anti-Patterns
❌ Three equally bold, equally colored primary buttons on a single screen — none is distinctive; none captures the Von Restorff benefit.

❌ Using random visual styling for emphasis throughout a document — distinctiveness is diluted when applied uniformly.
