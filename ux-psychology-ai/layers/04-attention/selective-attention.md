# L04-P01 — Selective Attention

**Layer:** 04-attention
**Evidence level:** A
**Type:** Empirical phenomenon
**Domain:** Cognitive psychology, attention

---

## Definition

Selective attention is the capacity to focus cognitive processing on one source of information while filtering out other simultaneous information. Humans cannot attend to all available interface information simultaneously — attention is necessarily selective.

---

## Research Basis

Selective attention research spans decades, from Cherry's (1953) cocktail party experiments demonstrating auditory attention filtering, to Treisman's (1960, 1964) attention models. The selective and limited nature of attention is one of the most fundamental and replicated phenomena in cognitive psychology.

**Evidence level A:** Foundational, extensively replicated.

---

## Why It Matters

Users will not see, read, or process everything on a screen. Their attention is directed — by salience, by task goals, by scanning habits. Design that assumes users will notice all content on a screen is based on a false premise.

---

## UI Implications

1. **Design for the expected attentional path, not for exhaustive reading.** Assume users will scan, not read. The most important information must be positioned and styled to be captured by a selective scan.
2. **Do not place critical information in locations that selective attention typically skips.** Banner blindness is a form of learned attention filtering — elements that look like ads are filtered even if they are not.
3. **One primary attention target per view.** If you want users to do one thing, make that one thing the clearest attentional target.
4. **Status changes must capture attention to be useful.** A status update in a location users are not attending to will be missed. (See L04-P03 Change Blindness)
5. **Reduce attentional competition.** Multiple high-salience elements compete for limited attentional resources, degrading performance on all of them.

---

## Anti-Patterns

❌ Critical error messages placed in locations users have learned to filter (banner-like headers, sidebar areas during task focus).

❌ Multiple high-contrast, animated, or large elements all competing for attention simultaneously.

❌ Designing for a "patient reader" when users are executing tasks under time pressure.

---

## Accessibility Considerations

- Users with ADHD or attention-related conditions may have different selective attention profiles — more vulnerable to distraction, more likely to miss items in expected locations.
- Avoid persistent motion in peripheral areas — it continuously captures involuntary attention and disrupts focused task attention.
